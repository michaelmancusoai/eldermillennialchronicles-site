For Parents

Questions about Tales of Italy

What is Tales of Italy?

Tales of Italy is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Italian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Islands (15 tales), Rome and Naples (11 tales) and Tuscany and the North (14 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Italy?

The Islands: Cola Pesce, Catherine and Her Fate, Firrazzanu and the Deaf Queen, The Abbot Who Had Nothing to Think About, Giufà and the Fly on the Judge’s Nose, The Man With One Bean, The Doctor’s Apprentice, One Hundred Looks at the King, The Cock Who Wanted to Be Pope, The Stones and the Bread, St Peter’s Mother and the Leek Leaf, The Girl Who Ate Only Sweets, The Story Without “Once Upon a Time”, Michele Fico Forgets His Name and Fra Ignazio’s Saddlebags. Rome and Naples: Godfather Wolf’s Frying Pan, Twelve Feet of Nose, The Old Miser Who Wanted a Wife Who Lived on Air, The Beggar’s Ewe, The Spoon in St Peter’s Bag, St Peter and the Ham, St Peter and the Missing Liver, The Cat Who Was Not Thanked, Goat-Face, The Months and Truthful Joseph. Tuscany and the North: Buchettino, The Wolf and the Crab, The Share of the Lashes, Pomo and the Black Horse, March and the Shepherd, The Blind Man and the Fig Tree, The Monkey and the Watered Wine, The Frying-Pan Wager, The Witches’ Figs, The Boy Who Mocked the Old Woman, The Fiddle, The Enchanted Tree, The Cobbler Who Killed Flies and The Language of Animals.

Where do the tales in Tales of Italy come from?

The Sicilian tales come from Giuseppe Pitrè, a Palermo doctor who filled twenty-five volumes with what his neighbours told him, and from Laura Gonzenbach, who wrote down tales in Messina for German readers in 1870. The Sardinian tales come from Francesco Mango’s little book of 1890 and from Gino Bottiglioni, a linguist who printed each tale in Sardinian with its teller’s name. The Roman tales and legends come from Rachel Busk, who collected them by word of mouth and printed them in 1874. The Abruzzese legends come from Antonio De Nino of Pratola Peligna. The Neapolitan tales come from Giambattista Basile’s Pentamerone of 1634. The northern tales come from Gherardo Nerucci and Vittorio Imbriani, from Idelfonso Nieri of Lucca, Isaia Visentini of Mantua, Domenico Bernoni of Venice, Giovanni Papanti of Livorno, Domenico Comparetti and Isabella Anderton, and three of them from far older books: Il Novellino, Franco Sacchetti and Giovanni Francesco Straparola. Many of these reached English readers first through Thomas Frederick Crane’s Italian Popular Tales of 1885.

What age is Tales of Italy for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Italy too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

Made by a father, checked by the two toughest critics in the house.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
