For Parents

Questions about Tales of Southeast Asia

What is Tales of Southeast Asia?

Tales of Southeast Asia is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Southeast Asian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Mouse-Deer’s Wits (15 tales), The Judge’s Mat (11 tales) and Under the Banyan (14 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Southeast Asia?

The Mouse-Deer’s Wits: The Tiger’s Bargain, The Sick Tiger King, The Flower-Pecker’s Promise, The Mouse-Deer and the Giant at the Fish Hut, The Two Wild Bulls, The Tiger and the King’s Gong, The Ape Who Asked to Be Stewed, The Monkey and the Turtle, The Hummingbird and the Carabao, The Monkeys and the Dragonflies, The Battle of the Crabs, The Greedy Crow, The Monkey Who Traded Up, The Dog Who Scratched in the Pig’s Place and The Elephant’s Bet. The Judge’s Mat: The Four Paws of the Cat, The Man in the Mango Tree, The Elephant’s Mouth, The False Fortune-Teller, The Clever Wife’s Riddle, The Crow Is White, The Donkey Who Walked with the Lion, Strife and Peace, Poison-Mouth, The Man with the Coconuts and The Stone on Trial. Under the Banyan: Cuội and the Banyan Tree, The Grain of Rice, The Messenger Who Became a Dung Beetle, Why the Buffalo Has No Upper Teeth, The Masters Who Wanted to Be Young, The Smith Who Wished to Be the Moon, The Sao Bird Who Told the Truth, The Dead Tiger’s Whiskers, Lemon-Grass Crag, The Gedembai Who Called Your Name, The Alan’s Bead, The Cow Who Wanted a Bath, The Hawk’s Lost Ring and The First Monkey.

Where do the tales in Tales of Southeast Asia come from?

Some of those writers were careful listeners. Walter Skeat took down Malay fables in the villages of the Malay Peninsula. Antony Landes collected more than a hundred Vietnamese tales, with the help of Vietnamese scholars. Adhémard Leclère translated the Lao and Khmer law-tales that judges once learned by heart. Dean Fansler, teaching in Manila, asked his Filipino students to write down the stories they had grown up with, and he printed each one with the name and home town of the student who told it. We have kept those names wherever we could. One of the tales in this book comes to us from José Rizal himself, who wrote it down in 1889, long before he became the hero of his country.

What age is Tales of Southeast Asia for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Southeast Asia too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
