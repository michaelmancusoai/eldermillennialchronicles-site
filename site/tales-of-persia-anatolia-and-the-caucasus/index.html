For Parents

Questions about Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus

What is Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus?

Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Persian, Anatolian and Caucasian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Hodja’s Donkey (13 tales), The Div at the Door (14 tales) and The Shah’s Scales (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus?

The Hodja’s Donkey: The Elephant’s Wife, The Fox Who Wooed for the Miller, The Fox and the Partridge, The Cat Who Stole the Milk, Eat, My Coat!, The Grey Wolf and the Badger, The Fox, the Eagle and the Wolf, The Bristle That Came Too Late, The Peg in the Ruins, The Three Strays, Baldhead and the Scanty-Beards, The Swallow and the Mosquito and The Rock of Wishes. The Div at the Door: The Man Who Went to Find His Luck, The Wolf-Aunt, The Colt Who Lost His Wings, The Maiden of the Sea, Three Brothers and the Div, The Language of Birds, The Fisherman’s Son, The Sparrow’s Warning, Roads and Shortcuts, The Serpent and the Peasant, Shah Abbas and the Cobbler Faraj, The Thorn-Gatherer Who Left God’s Country, The Sorcerer and His Pupil and Mullah Bazid’s Second Coat. The Shah’s Scales: Jamshid’s Pride, Amirani in Chains, A Wise Weaver, The Flying Throne, The Forty Lies, The Talking Door, The Virgin Queen, Rustam and the Div Akvan, The Cadi and the Jewel Bag, The Three Precepts, Shah Abbas and the Ring, Selling the Wind and Salman and Rostom.

Where do the tales in Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus come from?

Our tellings follow the versions written down between about 1840 and 1925. A. G. Seklemian, an Armenian teacher, collected tales around Erzurum. D. L. R. and E. O. Lorimer wrote down the tales they heard in Kerman and among the Bakhtiari tribes, almost word for word. Marjory Wardrop translated the Georgian, Mingrelian and Gurian tales, and Adolf Dirr gathered tales from all over the Caucasus. Peter Lerch heard Kurdish tellers, and the Kurdish scholar Mela Mahmud Bayazidi wrote down tales for Auguste Jaba. Cyrus Adler and Allan Ramsay listened in the coffee houses of Istanbul, and Henry Barnham translated a Turkish book of the Hodja’s stories. Helen Zimmern retold the Book of Kings for young readers. Alexander Chodzko wrote down the songs of the Turkmen hero Köroğlu as the Azerbaijani minstrels of northern Persia sang them, and a scholar named Samoylovich heard the tale of the forty lies in Khiva. In 1892 a student teacher wrote down the Azerbaijani tale of Shah Abbas and the singing cobbler from a teller named Ali Salim-oglu, and Albert von Le Coq heard the Uyghur tale of the peg in the ruins in the Turfan oasis, far to the east.

What age is Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Persia, Anatolia and the Caucasus too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
