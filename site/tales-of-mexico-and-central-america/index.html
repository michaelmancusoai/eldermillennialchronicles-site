Forty Mexican and Central American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of Mexico and Central America, told the old way: Uncle Rabbit and Uncle Coyote, the Dwarf of Uxmal, the Blowfly’s Errand, the Proud Lady’s Somersault, El Cadejo, and more.

Three books in one: Uncle Rabbit and Uncle Coyote, The House of the Dwarf and The Street of the Somersault, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Mexico and Central America

What is Tales of Mexico and Central America?

Tales of Mexico and Central America is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Mexican and Central American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: Uncle Rabbit and Uncle Coyote (12 tales), The House of the Dwarf (14 tales) and The Street of the Somersault (14 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Mexico and Central America?

Uncle Rabbit and Uncle Coyote: The House That Said Good Afternoon, Uncle Coyote Keeps School, Uncle Coyote Holds Up the World, The Third Prickly Pear, Coyote’s Fast, The Alligator Who Drank the Moon’s Milk, The Puma and the Bag of Chickens, The Jaguar Cub and the Woodcutter, The Squirrel and the Gopher, The Eagle and Her Comadre the Hare, The Fox’s Honey Hole and Uncle Rabbit Rides the Alligator. The House of the Dwarf: The Dwarf of Uxmal, The Ants Who Made a Milpa, What the Puma Told Her Cubs, The Rain God’s Servant, The Hunter Who Nursed the Wild Hogs, The Deer-Hunter’s Bride, The Hunter Who Ate the Snake, The Hunter and the Talking Tree, The Coyote Behind the Agave, The Blowfly’s Errand, The Boy and the Pot, The Deer-Folk, Compadre Lightning and Compadre Serpent and The Old Man at the Door. The Street of the Somersault: The King and the Boy Gathering Sticks, Pedro de Urdemales Sells a Money Tree, The Thief Who Was a Mule, The Lawyer’s Last Trick, The Apprentice Who Waited for Figs, The Mosquito Who Asked Who Was Bravest, The Duendes of La Loma, El Cotonudo, The Proud Lady’s Somersault, Dead Man’s Alley, El Cadejo, The Soldier Who Woke Up in Mexico, The Girl Who Drew a Train and Fihurití.

Where do the tales in Tales of Mexico and Central America come from?

Most of them were written down between 1911 and 1930 by people who went to listen: Franz Boas, who heard a Chatino man named Ezequiel Vázquez tell of the rabbit and the coyote; Paul Radin and Aurelio Espinosa, who gathered tales from all over Oaxaca; J. Alden Mason, who sat with Felipe and Eleno Aguilar in Azqueltán; Eric Thompson, who listened to the Maya of Belize; Adrián Recinos, who wrote down the stories of Guatemala; Eduard Conzemius, who lived among the Pech of Honduras; and Carl Hartman, who spent a year with the Pipil of El Salvador. Some were written by the tellers themselves: Isabel Ramírez Castañeda and a young man named Lucio wrote theirs in Nahuatl, in Milpa Alta, and Samuel Villalobo wrote his in Zapotec. The city legends come from Thomas Janvier, who heard them from the porters and old women of the capital, and the story of King Nezahualcóyotl from his own descendant, Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxóchitl, four hundred years ago.

What age is Tales of Mexico and Central America for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Mexico and Central America too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

Made by a father, checked by the two toughest critics in the house.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
