Tales of Native North America

Forty Native North American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of the many nations of Native North America, told the old way: Why the Possum’s Tail Is Bare, the Rabbit and the Tar Wolf, Iktomi and the Ducks, Coyote and the Humming-Birds, Qasiagssaq the Great Liar, and more.

Three books in one: Of Rabbits and Reckonings, Under the Wide Sky and Cedar, Salt and Ice, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Native North America

What is Tales of Native North America?

Tales of Native North America is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Native North American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: Of Rabbits and Reckonings (15 tales), Under the Wide Sky (13 tales) and Cedar, Salt and Ice (12 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Native North America?

Of Rabbits and Reckonings: Why the Possum’s Tail Is Bare, The Rabbit and the Tar Wolf, The Rabbit Goes Duck Hunting, How the Squirrel Got His Blanket, Two Lazy Hunters, How the Deer Got His Horns, Rabbit and the Fire in the Grass, Why the Rabbit Steals, How the Alligator’s Nose Was Broken, The Owl and the Little Yellow Mouse, The Panther and the Crane, The Opossum’s Silver Tail, The Raccoon and the Crabs, The Turtle and the Beaver and Wuchowsen, the Wind-Blower. Under the Wide Sky: Wihio and the Plums in the Water, Where the Mice Danced, The Turning Stones, Wihio Becomes a Buffalo, How the Coyote Danced with the Blackbirds, The Coyote and the Locust, The Coyote and the Ravens Who Raced Their Eyes, How the Turtle Duped the Coyote, Iktomi and the Ducks, Iktomi and the Muskrat, Iktomi and the Coyote, The Frogs and the Crane and Iktomi and the Turtle. Cedar, Salt and Ice: Grizzly Bear and Beaver, The Porcupine-Hunter, The Discontented Grass Plant, Txamsem and the Fishermen’s Bait, Beaver and Porcupine, Coyote and His Guests, Coyote and the Humming-Birds, Coyote and the Fisherman, The Raven Who Wanted a Wife, The Raven and the Goose, When the Ravens Could Speak and Qasiagssaq, the Great Liar.

Where do the tales in Tales of Native North America come from?

Every story in this book was told aloud for a very long time before anyone wrote it down, and most were written down between the 1880s and the 1920s. Some of the writers were from the nations whose stories they kept: Zitkála-Šá and Charles Eastman, who were Dakota; Arthur C. Parker, who was Seneca; William Jones, who was part Meskwaki; Henry Tate, who was Tsimshian; Louis Mitchell, who was Passamaquoddy. Others were outsiders who listened for years: James Mooney among the Cherokee, Frank Cushing at Zuni, George Bird Grinnell with the Cheyenne, James Teit, who married into the Nlaka’pamux, Roland Dixon among the Maidu, John Swanton in the Southeast and in Alaska, Edward Nelson on the Bering Strait, Charles Leland in Maine, and Knud Rasmussen in Greenland.

What age is Tales of Native North America for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Native North America too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

The Authors

Made by a father, checked by the two toughest critics in the house.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
