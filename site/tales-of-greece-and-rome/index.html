For Parents

Questions about Tales of Greece and Rome

What is Tales of Greece and Rome?

Tales of Greece and Rome is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Greek and Roman Myths and Legends, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Gods Are Watching (13 tales), Heroes and Their Flaws (13 tales) and The Roman Way (14 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Greece and Rome?

The Gods Are Watching: Arachne, Leto and the Frogs, Battus the Herdsman, Salmoneus, the Man Who Played Zeus, Narcissus and Echo, King Midas’s Ears, Tantalus, Dionysus and the Pirates, Laomedon’s Wages, Erysichthon’s Hunger, The Cranes of Ibycus, Phaethon and Baucis and Philemon. Heroes and Their Flaws: Archimedes and the Golden Crown, Atalanta’s Golden Apples, Bellerophon and Pegasus, Orpheus Looks Back, Sisyphus, Odysseus and the Cyclops, The Bag of Winds, Circe’s Swine, The Cattle of the Sun, The Boast of Ajax, Solon and Croesus, Damon and Phintias and Pyrrhus and the Roof Tile. The Roman Way: Tarpeia at the Gate, The Sibylline Books, Horatius at the Bridge, Menenius and the Belly, Cloelia Crosses the River, Coriolanus and His Mother, Cincinnatus and the Plough, The Schoolmaster of Falerii, The Geese of the Capitol, Brennus and the Scales, The Sword of Damocles, Fabricius and the Poisoner, Regulus Keeps His Word and Androcles and the Lion.

Where do the tales in Tales of Greece and Rome come from?

Every tale in this book is more than two thousand years old. Most of the Greek myths come from the Roman poet Ovid, who gathered them into one long poem called the Metamorphoses, about people who were changed into other things. Others come from Homer, the oldest poet of all; from Pindar, who wrote songs for athletes; from the poet Callimachus; and from Apollodorus, who wrote the old stories down in a plain handbook so that nobody would get them muddled. The Roman tales come mostly from Livy and Plutarch, with a few from Cicero and from Aulus Gellius, who collected odd stories the way some people collect stamps.

What age is Tales of Greece and Rome for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Greece and Rome too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
