For Parents

Questions about Tales of India

What is Tales of India?

Tales of India is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Indian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: Lessons for the Princes (13 tales), Good Counsel (15 tales) and Riddles in the Dark (12 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of India?

Lessons for the Princes: The Monkey and the Wedge, The Crane and the Crab, The Crow and the Golden Chain, The Lion and the Clever Hare, The Blue Jackal, The Camel Who Offered Himself, The Talkative Tortoise, The Three Fishes, Right-Mind and Wrong-Mind, The Foolish Friend, How the Birds Chose a King, The Frogs Who Rode the Snake and The Crows and the Owls. Good Counsel: The Crow and the Egret, How the Old Man’s Sons Were Duped, The Ungrateful Goat, The Monkey and the Jackal, The Loyal Mongoose, The Lion-Makers, The Weaver-Birds and the Monkeys, The Jackal, the Goat and the Wolf Who Judged, The Mouse Who Became a Tiger, The Three Rogues and the Goat, The Quarrel of the Quails, The Selfish Sparrow and the Houseless Crows, The Merchant of Seri, The Deer Who Skipped His Lessons and The Banyan Deer. Riddles in the Dark: The Goblin’s Riddle: The Poisoned Rice, The Ghost Who Was Afraid of Being Bagged, The Tiger, the Brahmin and the Jackal, Mighty-of-Mouth and Mighty-of-Hands, The Robbers Who Made Them Dance, Tit for Tat, The Ghost Who Stole a House, The Close Alliance, The Demon and the Curly Hair, The Jackal and the Iguana, The Beggar and the Five Pancakes and The King Who Would Not Bow.

Where do the tales in Tales of India come from?

The Panchatantra was written in Sanskrit, probably in the north-west of India, around two thousand years ago. The old original is lost; the tales here follow the version Arthur W. Ryder translated in 1925. Around the year 570 it was carried into Persian, then into Arabic as Kalila and Dimna, then into Hebrew and Latin, until half the world was telling it. The Hitopadesha is its Bengal cousin, from about a thousand years ago, and here follows Sir Edwin Arnold’s English of 1861. The Jataka tales are Buddhist, told in the old language called Pali; some were carved in stone at Bharhut and Sanchi over two thousand years ago. The goblin’s riddles come from Somadeva’s Ocean of Story, written in Kashmir in the eleventh century.

What age is Tales of India for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of India too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
