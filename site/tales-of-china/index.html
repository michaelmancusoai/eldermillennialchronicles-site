For Parents

Questions about Tales of China

What is Tales of China?

Tales of China is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Chinese Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Masters’ Fables (17 tales), Tales from the Village Gate (11 tales) and Strange Tales by Lamplight (12 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of China?

The Masters’ Fables: The Snipe and the Clam, The Fox Who Walked in Front of the Tiger, Drawing Legs on the Snake, Two Farmers of Song: the Stump and the Sprouts, The Frog in the Well, Learning to Walk in Handan, Two Fools of the Market: the Spear and Shield, and the Measuring String, Mr. Nanguo Plays the Pipes, The Fierce Dog and the Sour Wine, North to Reach the South, The Man Who Loved Seagulls, Rich Mr. Guo and Poor Mr. Xiang, The Monkey Who Caught Arrows, The Marquis Who Loved a Seabird, The Mantis, the Cicada and the Oriole, Hundun and the Seven Holes and The Duke Who Was Not Ill. Tales from the Village Gate: The Ten Straw Hats, The Roc and the Island of Gold, The Magic Cask, False Economy, The Moon-Cake, Stolen Garlic, The Miser and the Tiger, Ximen Bao and the River God’s Bride, Zhou Chu and the Three Evils, Kuafu Chases the Sun and The Monkey in the Buddha’s Hand. Strange Tales by Lamplight: Lord Ye Who Loved Dragons, Planting a Pear-Tree, The Daoist of Mount Lao, The Theft of a Duck, The Great Rat, The Tiger of Zhaocheng, Friendship with Foxes, Fox-Fire, The Ghost Who Was Sold as a Goat, The Weiqi Ghost, The Coffin by the River and The Ant on the Reed.

Where do the tales in Tales of China come from?

The fables of Book the First come from the writings of the philosophers and advisers of the Warring States period, between about 450 and 220 BCE, and from the collections that Liu Xiang put together in the first century BCE. Most of them have never been put into English in a version old enough to be free for everyone, so we have retold them straight from the old Chinese texts, with James Legge’s Mencius (1875) and Herbert Giles’s Zhuangzi (1889) at our elbow.

What age is Tales of China for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of China too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
