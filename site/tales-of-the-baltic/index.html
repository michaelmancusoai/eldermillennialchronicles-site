Tales of the Baltic

Forty Baltic Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of the Baltic, told the old way: the Old Man of Lake Ülemiste, How the Animals Dug the Daugava, Laima’s Shoes, the Night Spinners, Why the Stork’s Back Is Black, and more.

Three books in one: The Old Man of the Lake, Who Dug the River and The Stork’s Sack, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of the Baltic

What is Tales of the Baltic?

Tales of the Baltic is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Baltic Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Old Man of the Lake (13 tales), Who Dug the River (15 tales) and The Stork’s Sack (12 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of the Baltic?

The Old Man of the Lake: The Orphan’s Handmill, The Dwarf’s Christening, The Faithless Fisherman, The Man in Bast Shoes, The Miser’s Wooden Servants, Tiidu the Piper, The Spider and the Hornet, The Spirit of the Whirlwind, The Treasure at Kärdla, The Old Man of Lake Ülemiste, The Woodcutter’s Golden Rod, Emmu Lake and Virts Lake and The Ice Roof of the Lake. Who Dug the River: How the Animals Dug the Daugava, The Fox and the Jug, The Crane Who Taught the Fox to Fly, The Hare Who Laughed, The Catfish and the Burbot, The Fox and the Thrush, The Cuckoo and the Deaf Ploughman, The Owl Who Would Not Learn, Why the Wolf’s Mouth Is Black, The Horse Who Was Too Busy, The Miller Who Played the Bear, The Pot That Was Too Full, Laima’s Shoes, Why Nobody Knows Their Last Day and The Thrush Who Didn’t Give It Back. The Stork’s Sack: The Wolf Who Wanted to Bake Bread, The Owl on Watch, The Heron and the Crane, The Goat Who Lied, Why the Stork’s Back Is Black, The King Who Picked Up Three Pennies, The Crayfish Who Wanted Eyes Behind, The Man Who Gave Away His Belly, The Rich Meadow and the Poor Bog, The Old Man’s Night’s Lodging, The Night Spinners and The Cat and the Sparrow.

Where do the tales in Tales of the Baltic come from?

The Estonian tales come from the collections of Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald, put into German by Ferdinand Löwe in 1869 and 1881, and from Harry Jannsen’s legends, which reached English through William Forsell Kirby’s The Hero of Esthonia in 1895. The Latvian tales were gathered by Ansis Lerhis-Puškaitis between 1891 and 1903, by the writers of the journal Etnogrāfiskas ziņas, and in Latgale by Zofia Ulanowska, and were later reprinted, with their tellers’ names, by Pēteris Šmits. The Lithuanian tales come from Jonas Basanavičius’s four volumes of 1902 to 1905, and the last of them from August Schleicher, who heard it from Urtė Margematienė of Ragainė in 1856.

What age is Tales of the Baltic for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of the Baltic too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
