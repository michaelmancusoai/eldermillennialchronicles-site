Tales of Korea

Forty Korean Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of Korea, told the old way: Heungbu and Nolbu, the Tiger and the Dried Persimmon, the Magpie Bridge, the Sun and the Moon, the Goblins’ Golden Club, and more.

Three books in one: Greed and Gratitude, Tigers and Other Beasts and Goblins, Ghosts and Wise Judges, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Korea

What is Tales of Korea?

Tales of Korea is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Korean Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: Greed and Gratitude (13 tales), Tigers and Other Beasts (14 tales) and Goblins, Ghosts and Wise Judges (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Korea?

Greed and Gratitude: Heungbu and Nolbu, The Old Man in Plain Clothes, The Cat, the Dog and the Amber Bead, The Sluggard’s Cure, The Skull Under the Creeper, The Gold in the River, The Mirror from Seoul, The Friend Who Fed Your Family, The Examination Riddle, The Student Who Stayed Behind, Ondal the Fool, Half a Mirror and The Three Wise Sayings. Tigers and Other Beasts: The Tiger and the Dried Persimmon, The Rabbit and the Turtle, Bones for the Shad, The Tiger on the Ice, Who Is the Eldest?, A Husband for Miss Mole, The Bear Who Fought the Stone, Brother Tiger, The Magpie Bridge, The Unmannerly Tiger, Mr Long Body and Miss Thousand Feet, The Magpie’s New Coat, The Fox Who Lost His Tail and The Sun and the Moon. Goblins, Ghosts and Wise Judges: The Story Bought for a Hundred Strings, The Brave Magistrate, The Goblins’ Golden Club, The Horse That Knew the Way Home, The Toad in the Dark Jar, The King’s Letter, The Man Who Bought a Title, Chin and King Yama’s Messengers, The Pheasant and the Bell, The Sparrows and the Flies, The Woodcutter Who Watched the Game, The Ragged Boy at the Feast and The Son Who Raised His Hand.

Where do the tales in Tales of Korea come from?

Most of the folk tales were collected around 1900. A few Western writers living in Korea put them into English: Horace Allen in 1889, Homer Hulbert in his magazine The Korea Review (1901 to 1906) and in a book for children (1925), James Gale in 1913, and William Griffis in 1911. Hulbert’s magazine also printed tales written down by Korean contributors, among them Ko Pyung Ik and Yi Chong-won. In the 1920s the Korean storyteller Bang Jeong-hwan retold old tales for children in his magazine Eorini, whose name means “children”.

What age is Tales of Korea for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Korea too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
