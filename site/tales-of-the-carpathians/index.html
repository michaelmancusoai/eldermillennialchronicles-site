Tales of the Carpathians

Forty Carpathian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of the Hungarians, the Transylvanian Saxons and the Romanians, told the old way: the Golden-Fleeced Lamb, the Little Dumpling, Why the Stork Has No Tail, Baba Dochia, the Wolf Who Would Be Mayor, and more.

Three books in one: The Golden Lamb, The Nightingale’s Twelve Tunes and When God Walked the Earth, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of the Carpathians

What is Tales of the Carpathians?

Tales of the Carpathians is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Carpathian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Golden Lamb (13 tales), The Nightingale’s Twelve Tunes (13 tales) and When God Walked the Earth (14 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of the Carpathians?

The Golden Lamb: The Golden-Fleeced Lamb, The Little Dumpling, The Reward of Boasting, The Little Hen and the Sloe, Luck and Bliss, The Student and the Loaf, Truth and Falsehood, The Castle of Luck, The Emperor Who Said There Was No God, The King and the Two Maidens, The Just Wage, Two Neighbours and Their Sheep and The Duckling’s Journey. The Nightingale’s Twelve Tunes: The Nightingale and the Turtle-Dove, The Nightingale at the King’s Table, The Titmouse Who Didn’t Wait, The Cuckoo’s Broken Promise, The Nosy Old Woman, The Hornet’s Cardboard Gift, The Bee and the Spider, Why the Stork Has No Tail, The Stork and the Tomtit, The Hoopoe Who Wanted Better, The Late Thistle-Finch, The Maize Loaf and the Wheat Loaf and The Girl Who Spun from the Rubbish. When God Walked the Earth: The Gad-Fly and the Lazy Cowherd, The Woman Who Hid the Loaf, The Sower’s Wheat, The Boastful Thrush, Baba Dochia, The Rich Man’s Granary, The Wolf Who Would Be Mayor, The Lazy Dog, The Goat and the Snake, The Stingy Farmer’s Stable, The Son Who Turned His Mother Away, The Steward’s Work-Party, The Carter Who Answered to His Name and The Rat Who Went to Find God.

Where do the tales in Tales of the Carpathians come from?

The Hungarian tales come from the collections of László Arany, György Gaal and Arnold Ipolyi, and from W. Henry Jones and Lewis Kropf’s English Folk-Tales of the Magyars of 1889. The Csángó emperor comes from Antal Horger’s book of the tales of the Seven Villages, printed in 1908. The Transylvanian Saxon tales come from Josef Haltrich, who gathered them around Schäßburg and printed them in 1856. The Romanian bird and beast stories come from Moses Gaster’s Rumanian Bird and Beast Stories of 1915, and from the great folklore books of Simion Florea Marian; the village tales from Petre Ispirescu of Bucharest; the two Moldavian tales from Elena Niculiță-Voronca, who printed them in 1903; and Baba Dochia from two German brothers, Arthur and Albert Schott, who heard her story in the Banat in the 1840s.

What age is Tales of the Carpathians for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of the Carpathians too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

The Authors

Made by a father, checked by the two toughest critics in the house.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
