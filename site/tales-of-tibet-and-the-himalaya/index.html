Forty Tibetan and Himalayan Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of Tibet and the Himalaya, told the old way: the Stone Lion, the Kyang and the Rope, the Four Friends, the Two Little Cats and the Handre, the Headman’s Wise Wife, and more.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya

What is Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya?

Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Tibetan and Himalayan Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Stone Lion’s Mouth (13 tales), Brother Hare’s Rope (14 tales) and The Headman’s Wife (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya?

The Stone Lion’s Mouth: The Stone Lion, The Pewter Vase, The Bow by the Pool, The Pit-Digger’s Neighbour, The Man Who Shook Down the Monkeys, The Jealous Fox, The Seven Dumplings, The Hundred Ounces of Silver, The Turquoise on the Dragon’s Neck, The Golden Duck, The Cony in Bad Company, The Skin That Cooked Supper and Dewa and the Ogress’s Children. Brother Hare’s Rope: The Tiger Who Wanted a House, The Kyang and the Rope, The Frog Who Sharpened the Crow’s Beak, The Cat Who Took Up Prayer, The King of the Mice, The Toad Who Ate Tigers, The Jackal Who Moved In, The Monkey Who Called the Wind, The Old Man in the Wedding Drum, The Tiger and the Donkey, Sitoli and the Seventh Grain, The Hare Who Shared Out the Pack, The Four Friends and The Two Little Cats and the Handre. The Headman’s Wife: Sankhadhar Sakhwa and the Sand of Gold, The Headman’s Wise Wife, The Donkey and the Rock on Trial, The Lazy Son Who Found Things, The Eastern Hero and the Western Hero, The Monkeys Who Fished for the Moon, The Juggler’s Lesson, The Grey Old Woman, The Violinist and the King Who Wanted Everything, When the Rivers Met, The Fish King’s Gift, The Mouse Who Had a Tiger for a Son and The Bride Who Saved the Rice.

Where do the tales in Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya come from?

Our tellings follow the versions written down a little over a hundred years ago: by W. F. O’Connor in central Tibet (1906); by A. L. Shelton, an American doctor in Kham (1925); by Rinchen Lhamo, a woman of Kham, in We Tibetans (1926); by C. de Beauvoir Stocks among the Lepcha (1925); at Leh in Ladakh (1899); by Pandit Ganga Datt Upreti, a scholar of Kumaon who wrote down what the old people of his own hills told him (1894 and 1897); and in the old chronicle of the Newars of the Kathmandu Valley, put into English in 1877. Upreti, like Shelton, set each of his tales beside a proverb of the hills. The three scripture fables are retold from Schiefner and Ralston (1882) and I. J. Schmidt (1843).

What age is Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Tibet and the Himalaya too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
