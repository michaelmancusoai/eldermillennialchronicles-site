Tales of South America

Forty South American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way

These are forty old tales of South America, told the old way: the Jaguar’s Bone Flute, the Curupira’s Roast, Ceiuci the Hungry Old Woman, the Fox Who Went to the Feast in the Sky, the Free Colt and the Mule, and more.

Three books in one: The Tortoise and the Jaguar, The Forest Is Listening and The Fox of the High Country, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of South America

What is Tales of South America?

Tales of South America is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty South American Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Tortoise and the Jaguar (12 tales), The Forest Is Listening (15 tales) and The Fox of the High Country (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of South America?

The Tortoise and the Jaguar: The Jaguar’s Bone Flute, The Tortoise Who Danced Away, The Jaguar Sent to the Sun for Water, The Deer and the Jaguar Build a House, The Jaguar’s Travelling Companion, The Jaguar Who Wrestled Fire, The Turtle Who Lent His Hooves, The Woman Who Outwitted Two Jaguars, The Turtle Who Rolled Instead of Working, The Turtle and the Opossum’s Fast, The Candle-fly and the Lost Hunter and The Birds Who Mocked Everyone. The Forest Is Listening: The Evil-Eye Bird, The Curupira’s Roast, The Feast Nobody Cooked, The Man Who Claimed the Jaguar’s Kill, The Gourd That Must Be Half Full, The Boys Who Ran from Work, The Girl Who Stayed Home, The Boy Who Said Her Name, The Wife Who Taught Her Husband to Hunt, Ceiuci, the Hungry Old Woman, The Lucky Pot, The Honey Wife, The Lazy Man and the Crab Baskets, The Hornets and the Gold and The Man Who Wanted to Lasso God. The Fox of the High Country: The Puma and the Fox, The Fox Who Went to the Feast in the Sky, The Goose and the Sack of Birds, The Grub Who Grew Proud, The Tortoise and the Thorn Tree, The Greedy Brother, The Shepherd’s Last Song, The Fox and the Vulture, The Fox and the Horsefly, The Honey-Gatherer Who Carried Fire, The Traro and the Vulture, The Puma Who Played Dead and The Free Colt and the Mule.

Where do the tales in Tales of South America come from?

Most of these tellings were written down between 1870 and 1916. Some came from writers who belonged to the tradition: Adolfo Vienrich of Tarma printed the Quechua fables of his own valley, and Sílvio Romero gathered the tales of his own country. Others were taken down word for word from named tellers: Lourenço Maciel Parente, a canoe steersman at Santarém; Mayuluaipu and Akuli of the Pemon; Yambási of the Chiriguano; Segundo Jara Calvun of the Pehuenche. We have kept their names, because the stories were theirs.

What age is Tales of South America for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of South America too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
