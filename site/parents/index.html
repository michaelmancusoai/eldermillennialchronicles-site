For Parents

Questions parents ask before reading these aloud.

Straight answers about how frightening the tales are, what age they suit, and why the endings are the old ones.

Are these stories too scary for my child?

They are frightening the way the old stories were, and never gruesome. Nothing violent is shown or described: every tale stops one breath before the worst moment, and the final plate always leaves a clear gap.

Children fill that gap with exactly as much as they can handle. Most six-year-olds hear “Exactly what you think” and supply something milder than any illustration would show. If your child is sensitive, start with the fables in Book the First, which are the gentlest.

What age is The Elder Millennial Chronicles for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud by a grown-up. Confident readers of 9 and up can read it alone.

Each tale is about three minutes aloud, so one tale makes a complete bedtime story.

Why use the original endings instead of the happy ones?

Because the original endings carry the lesson. A Red Riding Hood rescued by a woodsman teaches that someone will always come; Perrault’s, with no woodsman, teaches a child not to tell a stranger where she is going.

The old storytellers told these tales to keep children safe, and the consequence is what made them work. This book puts the consequence back, and states the moral plainly at the top of the page so no child misses it.

Is there a woodsman in this Little Red Riding Hood?

No. This telling follows Charles Perrault’s version of 1697, which has no woodsman at all, and the story says so out loud.

How should I read these aloud?

Slowly, with the voices, and stop at the bad part.

The tales are written for a grown-up’s voice: short paragraphs, real dialogue and refrains children start saying along with you. When you reach the last line, let the silence sit. Then ask the question at the end of each tale’s page on this site, such as “Why didn’t anybody come the third time?”

Where do the tales come from?

From Aesop, Jean de La Fontaine, Charles Perrault, the Brothers Grimm, Hans Christian Andersen, Heinrich Hoffmann, the Greek myths and the old English and Norwegian folk tales.

Each tale’s page on this site names its source, and the back of the book has a full note on the old versions. The words are new; the lessons are not.
