For Parents

Questions about Tales of the Nordic Lands

What is Tales of the Nordic Lands?

Tales of the Nordic Lands is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Nordic Myths and Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Hall of Oaths (13 tales), Under the Troll Hill (14 tales) and The Edge of the Cold Sea (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of the Nordic Lands?

The Hall of Oaths: Gefjun Ploughs Away a Kingdom, The Builder of Asgard’s Wall, Thor’s Goats, Thor and Utgarda-Loki, Idunn’s Apples, Alvis the All-Knowing, Sif’s Hair and Loki’s Wager, Hrungnir’s Boast, The Otter’s Ransom, Thrym Steals the Hammer, The Binding of Fenrir, The Guest Between the Fires and The One Who Would Not Weep. Under the Troll Hill: Why the Sea Is Salt, The Husband Who Minded the House, Gertrude’s Bird, Why the Bear Has a Stumpy Tail, Nail Soup, Well Done and Ill Paid, Kitta Grau and the Evil One, The Cat on the Dovrefell, The Trolls in Hedale Wood, The Giant Who Hid His Heart, St Peter and the Two Women, The Boy Who Scared the Stallo, Yuletide Specters and The Lady of Pintorp. The Edge of the Cold Sea: The Girl Who Stepped on Bread, The Wonderful Pot, The Merman, The Stallo’s Shy Bride, The Singing Contest, The Nisse Who Killed the Cow, The Silver Dish, The Fir Tree, The Wolf Sings, The Mermaid’s Reward, The Wild Animals and the Tame Ones, The Raven of Skíðastaðir and The Giant’s Three Gifts.

Where do the tales in Tales of the Nordic Lands come from?

The myths follow Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, put into English by Rasmus B. Anderson and Arthur G. Brodeur, and the old poems of the Poetic Edda, translated by Henry Adams Bellows. The Norwegian tales follow Asbjørnsen and Moe in the translations of George Webbe Dasent; the Swedish ones come from Hofberg, Bondeson, Djurklou and the Swedish Fairy Book of Clara Stroebe. The Danish tales come from Svend Grundtvig, Evald Tang Kristensen and Hans Christian Andersen; the Icelandic legends from Jón Árnason, translated by George Powell and Eiríkr Magnússon; the Faroese legend from the collections of V. U. Hammershaimb, by way of William Craigie; the Finnish tales from the Kalevala of Elias Lönnrot, translated by W. F. Kirby, and from Parker Fillmore; and the Sámi tales from the collection J. A. Friis made in Finnmark, Troms and the north of Sweden (1871), most of them written down from the people’s own telling, and from J. C. Poestion’s collection of 1886, retold here from the Norwegian and the German.

What age is Tales of the Nordic Lands for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of the Nordic Lands too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
