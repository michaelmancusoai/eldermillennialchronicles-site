For Parents

Questions about Tales of the Steppe and Siberia

What is Tales of the Steppe and Siberia?

Tales of the Steppe and Siberia is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Folk Tales of the Steppe and Siberia, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Khan’s Hall (13 tales), The Long Road (14 tales) and The Cold Moon (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of the Steppe and Siberia?

The Khan’s Hall: The Letter from the Sky, The Three Brothers and the Lost Camel, The Blind Man’s Claim, The Khan’s Clever Daughter, The Khan’s Life-Stone, The Poor Man and His Luck, The Hunter Who Would Leave None, The Golden Mountain, The Merchant’s Son, The Clay Jewel, The Bat Who Belonged to Nobody, The Corncrake’s Secret and The Prince’s Last Word. The Long Road: Good and Evil on the Road, Zhirenshe the Wise, The Fox and the Quail, The Old Man and the Forest Spirit, The Hare’s Letter, The Owl and the Rope of Sunlight, The Hedgehog Judge, The Camel and the Deer, The Marmot’s Well, The Rich Man Who Became a Marmot, The Wise Parrot, The Wolf, the Fox and a Lump of Fat, The Three Companions and the Goose and The Orphan and the Wolf-King. The Cold Moon: The Owl Who Hid the Warm Land, The Girl Who Hid from the Moon, The Children in the Stranger’s Boat, Raven and the Sea-Spirit’s Gifts, Wolves and Men, The Wood-Master’s Sledge, The Old Man’s Clever Daughter, The Girls in the Man-Eater’s Boat, The Boy Who Went Looking for His Father, The Hare Who Kicked the Sun into the Sky, The Stingy Reindeer-Owners, The Sea-Spirit and the Chief’s Daughter and The Herd-Owner Who Turned to Stone.

Where do the tales in Tales of the Steppe and Siberia come from?

In the 1800s scholars began to write them down. Wilhelm Radloff, a German who spent his life among the Turkic peoples of Siberia, took down Kazakh and Tatar tales word for word from the people who told them. Bernhard Jülg printed the Mongol and Kalmyk tales of the Siddhi-Kür and of King Ardshi-Bordshi, and Rachel Busk put them into English in 1873. Grigory Potanin and Nikolai Katanov, who was himself a Khakas from the Abakan steppe, filled whole volumes with the tales of the Kazakhs, Mongols, Buryats, Altaians, Tuvans and Tofa, and Vasily Verbitsky, who spent most of his life in the Altai mountains, wrote down the Altai stories of how the world began. In the far north, Waldemar Bogoras and Gerhard von Maydell wrote down the tales of the Chukchi, the Even and the Yukaghir, and Fillingham Coxwell gathered many of these into one large English book in 1925.

What age is Tales of the Steppe and Siberia for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of the Steppe and Siberia too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
