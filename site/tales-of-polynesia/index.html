Three books in one: The Children of Māui and Hina, Sharks, Spirits and Small Creatures and Chiefs and Commoners, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Polynesia

What is Tales of Polynesia?

Tales of Polynesia is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Polynesian Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Children of Māui and Hina (13 tales), Sharks, Spirits and Small Creatures (14 tales) and Chiefs and Commoners (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Polynesia?

The Children of Māui and Hina: Rata’s Canoe, Māui and the Fire of Mahuika, Tiʻitiʻi and the Earthquake God, Why the Weeds Grow Back, The Brothers Who Cut the Fish, Kalokalo and the Sun’s Gift, Ina and the Smooth-Talking Thief, Hina and Kuna of the Wailuku, Rata and the Boar Chase, The Twins Who Went to the Sky, Tāwhaki and the Rude In-Laws, Toa and Pale and The Sweet Potato That Rolled Home. Sharks, Spirits and Small Creatures: Punia and the King of Sharks, The Rat and the Octopus, Kahukura and the Fairy Net, The Swamphen and the Giant Clam, The Ant and the Rat, Fakapatu and the Giant of Tofua, Kaehu and the Man-Eating Shark, The Bat Who Borrowed Wings, Tasi and the Owl, Te Kanawa and the Fairies, Sina and the Eel, The Ironwood Tree, The Veka and the Kale and Hanaʻaumoe the Flatterer. Chiefs and Commoners: Kae and the Pet Whale, The Plover’s Daughter, The Fisherman Who Gave Away Ten Fish, Māui and Irawaru, Kaululāʻau, the Prince Who Pulled Up Trees, Takarangi’s Water, Taʻilaʻila, the Boy Without Arms or Legs, Kihapiʻilani and the Bowl of Brine, The Thief Who Owned a Cave, Hiro and King Puna’s Trees, The Woman Who Pushed Up the Sky, Pakaʻa’s Wind Gourd and The Rattling Bones of Hua.

Where do the tales in Tales of Polynesia come from?

The Hawaiian tales come mostly from writers who were Hawaiian themselves: King David Kalākaua (1888); Emma Nakuina, who wrote about her own islands (1904); and the scholars S. M. Kamakau, J. Kepelino and S. N. Haleʻole, whose writings Abraham Fornander collected and which were printed in English and Hawaiian side by side from 1916. The Māori tales follow Sir George Grey’s book of 1855, which was drawn from writings by Māori authors, most of all the Te Arawa scholar Te Rangikāheke; one was told by the Waikato chief Te Wherowhero. The Samoan tales come from George Turner (1884) and from O. Sierich, who wrote down Samoan evening stories, fāgogo, from named Samoan storytellers in 1890 and 1891; one of them told him plainly that fāgogo were made-up stories, told for pleasure. The Tongan tales come from E. W. Gifford’s collection of 1924, the Cook Islands tales from William Wyatt Gill (1876), much of whose material came from a Mangaian priest, and the Marquesan, Niuean and Tokelauan tales from articles printed by the Polynesian Society between 1895 and 1923. The Society Islands tales come from Teuira Henry’s Ancient Tahiti (1928), as written down by Aita of Porapora (1825) and the Raʻiātean scholars Ara-mouʻa and Vara (1818).

What age is Tales of Polynesia for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Polynesia too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
