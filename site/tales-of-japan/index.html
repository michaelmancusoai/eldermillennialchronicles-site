For Parents

Questions about Tales of Japan

What is Tales of Japan?

Tales of Japan is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Japanese Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Heavy Basket (14 tales), The Tanuki’s Grin (14 tales) and Ghosts and Wonders (12 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Japan?

The Heavy Basket: The Tongue-Cut Sparrow, The Ash That Made the Trees Bloom, The Old Man’s Lump, The Fountain of Youth, The Price of a Smell, The Moon Princess’s Impossible Gifts, Benkei’s Thousandth Sword, The Last Arrow, Urashima Taro, The Shark-Man’s Tears, The Three Potted Trees, The Waterfall of Yoro, The Man Who Wanted Forever and The Old Woman and the Rolling Dumpling. The Tanuki’s Grin: The Monkey and the Crab, The Badger’s Money, The Fox Cub and the Old Tanuki, The Jellyfish and the Monkey, The Lucky Tea-Kettle, The Man Who Laughed at Foxes, The Hunter Who Trusted His Eyes, The Monkey Who Saved His Own Skin, The Kappa’s Promise, The Turban Shell, The Two Frogs, The Firefly Princess, The Bee in the Web and Chin-Chin Kobakama. Ghosts and Wonders: The Face in the Well, Issun-boshi, the One-Inch Boy, The Boy Who Drew Cats, The Goblin Spider, The Ogre’s Arm, The Hag of Adachigahara, Shippeitaro, The Priest Who Dreamed He Was a Carp, The Hundred Ghosts, The Devil He Made Himself, The Wife He Left and Kwashin Koji’s Picture.

Where do the tales in Tales of Japan come from?

Our tellings follow the English versions made a little over a hundred years ago. Yei Theodora Ozaki, who grew up between two languages, retold the best-loved children’s tales in 1903 and 1909. The publisher Hasegawa Takejirō of Tokyo printed them on soft crêpe paper, with pictures by Japanese artists and English words by Lafcadio Hearn, Basil Hall Chamberlain, David Thomson and others. Hearn’s own books, from Glimpses of Unfamiliar Japan to A Japanese Miscellany, gave us most of the ghosts, and the naturalist R. Gordon Smith wrote down more from the people who told them to him. A. B. Mitford and William Elliot Griffis, who lived in the country in the 1860s and 1870s, wrote down many tales just as they heard them, with the old hard endings left in.

What age is Tales of Japan for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Japan too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
