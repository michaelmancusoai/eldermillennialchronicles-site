For Parents

Questions about Tales of France & the Low Countries

What is Tales of France & the Low Countries?

Tales of France & the Low Countries is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty French and Low Countries Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Fox’s Court (13 tales), The Fairy’s Price (12 tales) and The Drowned Bells (15 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of France & the Low Countries?

The Fox’s Court: Bruin and the Honey-Tree, Tybert in the Priest’s Barn, The Price Written on the Hoof, Drakestail, The Blackbird and the Fox, The Sick Wolf, The Wolf, the Snail and the Wasps, The Two Goats, The Monkey and the Cat, The Wolf in the Pear Oven, The Mouse Who Asked Nicely, The Heron Who Waited for Better and Monsieur Seguin’s Goat. The Fairy’s Price: When the Long Winter Came, Weather to Order, The Divided Horse-Cloth, The Bird Who Kept the Orchard, A Lost Paradise, Misère and the Walnut Tree, Beauty and the Beast, Prince Darling, The Fair One with Golden Locks, The Three Wishes, Bellah and the Groac’h and Blondine. The Drowned Bells: The Lady of Stavoren, The Drowned City of Ys, The Stones of Plouhinec, The Palace Under the Dunes, The Piper and the Korrigans, Teuz and the Ashes, The Siren of La Fresnaye, The White Inn, The Ghost’s Supper, The Landmark Removed, Kludde, The Fiddler Tricked, The Three Water-Maidens of Jupille, Melusina of the Bock and The Flying Dutchman.

Where do the tales in Tales of France & the Low Countries come from?

The beast tales come from the Flemish story of Reynard, printed in English by William Caxton in 1481; from the fables of Jean de La Fontaine, published between 1668 and 1694; and from Jean-François Bladé, a Gascon who wrote down the tales of his neighbours and named every teller and village. Alphonse Daudet gave us Monsieur Seguin’s goat. The bargain tales come from the old fabliaux, from Charles Perrault, Madame d’Aulnoy, Madame Leprince de Beaumont and the Comtesse de Ségur, and from the farm tales gathered by Paul Sébillot and his fellow collectors in every province.

What age is Tales of France & the Low Countries for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of France & the Low Countries too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.

The Authors

Made by a father, checked by the two toughest critics in the house.

Michael A. Mancuso made these books for his own children, Gigi and Enzo, who helped decide which endings were scary enough and which jokes were funny enough. The result is a book you can read to a six-year-old tonight and still enjoy yourself the fortieth time through.
