For Parents

Questions about Tales of Arabia and the Nights

What is Tales of Arabia and the Nights?

Tales of Arabia and the Nights is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Tales of Arabia and the Nights, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Sealed Bottle (12 tales), The Olive Jar (15 tales) and The Black Tent (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Arabia and the Nights?

The Sealed Bottle: The Fisherman and the Jinni, The Prince and the Ghoul, The Dervish’s Ointment, The Black Stones on the Mountain, The Jewels of the Brass City, Abu Kir and Abu Sir, The Envier and the Envied, The Ebony Horse, Ijbeyneh and the Ghoul’s Mountain, The Camels’ Grievance, The Treasure Under the Fountain and The Dog’s Dish of Gold. The Olive Jar: Ali Cogia’s Olives, The Barmecide’s Feast, The Donkey Who Was a Man, Juha’s Peg, The Little Hunchback, The Drop of Honey, The City of Cheats, The Bundle Too Cheap, Abu Nuwas and the Judge Who Believed in Dreams, Stretch Your Legs to Your Quilt, The Old Man’s Figs, The Two Beggars and the Roast Fowl, The Boaster and the Skulls, The Judge’s Sack of Earth and Jaafar and the Beanseller. The Black Tent: The Wolf and the Fox, The Lion Cub and the Carpenter, The Woodseller’s Candles, The Mouse and the Weasel, Sa’id and Lulu, Why the Dog Is Tied, Hunayn’s Boots, Hatim’s Horse, Hatim’s Guests, The Goatherd’s Four Goats, Daher and the Mare, Ma’n and the Cucumber Man and Ahikar and the Ungrateful Nephew.

Where do the tales in Tales of Arabia and the Nights come from?

Most of our tellings follow the English versions made between about 1835 and 1913. Edward William Lane, who lived in Cairo for years, translated the Nights from Arabic for families. John Payne translated the whole of it a generation later, including the animal fables and the short desert stories that Lane left out. Andrew Lang retold the tales that Galland had heard from Hanna Diyab of Aleppo. James Edward Hanauer, who was born in Jerusalem and lived there all his life, wrote down the tales he heard from the farmers and townspeople around him. Fatallah Sayeghir of Aleppo, who travelled among the desert tribes, told the story of Daher and the mare in his own memoir. Agnes Smith Lewis translated the Arabic version of Ahikar. Three tales come from the far south of Arabia, written down word for word in German by scholars from Vienna: two from Abd el-Hadi ben Marzuq, a sailor of the al-Mahra coast, who told them in Mehri, a South Arabian language of its own and not a kind of Arabic; and one from Mhammed ben Selim, a Bedouin frankincense worker of the Dhofar mountains.

What age is Tales of Arabia and the Nights for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Arabia and the Nights too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
