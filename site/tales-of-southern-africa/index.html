These are forty old tales of Southern Africa, told the old way: Five Heads, Unanana and the One-Tusked Elephant, Demane and Demazana, The Bird That Made Milk, Masilo and Masilonyane, and more.

Three books in one: The Jackal’s Road, Beyond the Last Hut and The Bird That Tells, with one hundred and twenty ink-and-watercolour plates.

For Parents

Questions about Tales of Southern Africa

What is Tales of Southern Africa?

Tales of Southern Africa is a book in The Elder Millennial Chronicles: “Forty Southern African Folk Tales, Told the Old Way.” Every tale opens with its moral and keeps the consequences of the oldest versions. It is three books in one: The Jackal’s Road (15 tales), Beyond the Last Hut (12 tales) and The Bird That Tells (13 tales), with 120 ink-and-watercolour plates, three for each tale.

Which tales are in Tales of Southern Africa?

The Jackal’s Road: The Lion Who Would Not Listen to His Mother, The Lion Who Could Fly, The Two-Headed Python, The Lion and the Baboon, The Leopard and the Ram, The Dove, the Heron and the Jackal, The Hyena Who Played Dead, The Lazy Woman, The Tortoise and the Baboons, The Giraffe and the Tortoise, The Zebra Stallion, The Jackal Who Would Not Dig, The Elephant Who Ate the Beans, Lion and Little Jackal and Isuro and Gudu. Beyond the Last Hut: Five Heads, Untombinde and the River Monster, Unanana and the One-Tusked Elephant, Dance Like That!, Demane and Demazana, The Wonderful Feather, The Disobedient Boy and the Big Snake, Kenkebe, The Bird That Made Milk, Kgolodikane, The Little Wise Woman and The Rock with Eyes. The Bird That Tells: The Hyrax Who Sent for His Tail, The Hyena and the Moon, The Baboons and the Leopard, Uthlakanyana and the Iguana’s Whistle, The Man Who Threw Away His Bread, Be-Thankful, the Dog Who Went Home, The Wife Carved from Wood, The Wonderful Horns, Simbukumbukwana, The Boy with the Moon on His Chest, The Tortoise Nobody Ate, Seetetelane and the Ostrich-Egg Wife and Masilo and Masilonyane.

Where do the tales in Tales of Southern Africa come from?

Our tellings follow the English versions of that time. Henry Callaway printed the Zulu tales in 1868 with the isiZulu on one page and the English on the other, just as his tellers gave them. Xhosa tellers wrote down the tales that G. M. Theal published in 1882, and a grandson of Chief Moroko wrote down the Barolong tale of the milk-bird. Édouard Jacottet gathered the Sotho tales in Lesotho, from Sesotho tellers, and published them in 1908. The Nama fables came from the Nama-language notebooks of G. Krönlein, who lived among the Nama for many years; W. H. I. Bleek translated them in 1864. R. S. Rattray wrote down the Chewa tales in Malawi in 1907, in Chinyanja, and Alice Werner heard the tale of the elephant and the beans from a teacher at Blantyre. J. Torrend wrote down the Lenje tales of a storyteller named Mumba, who sang her songs as she told them. Henri-A. Junod, a Swiss teacher at Delagoa Bay, wrote down the Ronga and Thonga tales of southern Mozambique in 1897 and 1913, songs and all, and noted that a tale must never be told at noon.

What age is Tales of Southern Africa for?

Ages 6 to 12, read aloud with a grown-up. Each tale takes about three minutes to read. It opens with its moral, and the third plate stops one breath before the worst part, so the ending is understood rather than shown.

Is Tales of Southern Africa too scary for young children?

The tales keep their old endings: the greedy are punished and the careless pay for it. Nothing is shown past the turning point, and every tale says its lesson plainly first. If your child is very sensitive, read a tale yourself before reading it aloud. There is more on this in the For Parents section. Read the answers for parents.
